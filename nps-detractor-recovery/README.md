# NPS Detractor Recovery

An n8n automation that turns low NPS survey scores into same-day outreach. It collapses a noisy stream of survey webhooks into one assembled response, alerts the support team in Slack, identifies who the respondent actually is, opens a tracked escalation case, and runs a stateful follow-up sequence that invites the client to book time with a real person.

## Why It Is Hard

Two things make this harder than it looks. First, the survey tool's webhook is extremely verbose: it fires on every click and every answer, so a single survey produces several events, and the first one can carry a score the respondent later changes. Second, respondents are not required to be tied to a customer record. A survey can be completed from any address, so figuring out which client a detractor belongs to takes layered matching, not a simple lookup.

## Architecture

1. **Trigger.** When a client clicked a score on an NPS survey in Delighted, a webhook fired immediately, before the respondent had finished. A score of six or below marked a detractor. Zapier stayed in place as the webhook sender out of Delighted, because moving the trigger off it would have required building a new developer integration. n8n took over from the webhook onward.
2. **Deduplicate and assemble the response.** The workflow treated the stream of events as one response to be assembled in Supabase. On each event it checked for a record of that respondent's survey within a recent window, using the metadata Delighted attached. The first event created a record with the initial score and started a 30-minute timer, long enough for a slow respondent to finish both follow-up questions. Later events were compared against the stored record, and anything new, such as a changed score or an answered question, was merged in field by field, while events that added nothing were discarded. When the timer expired with no further changes, the workflow continued with the fully assembled response. A respondent who changed their mind from a three to a five was not treated as a detractor.
3. **Alert the team.** The assembled response was posted to a dedicated Slack channel monitored by the support managers, showing the score and exactly what the client had written, with a link straight to the escalation case. The first version of the alert fired immediately with only the score. The director of client support wanted the respondent's comments visible in Slack itself, and those arrive after the score, so the alert moved behind the settling timer.
4. **Identify the client.** The workflow ran a multi-pass match from the most certain signal to the loosest: an exact match of the email domain against known client website domains, then an exact match of the email against Salesforce contacts, then first and last name matching including variants such as Tony and Anthony, then last-name-only matching to surface candidates. This identified the client for over 90% of detractors. The few that stayed unmatched still received outreach.
5. **Open a tracked escalation.** Every detractor got an escalation case in Salesforce, tied to the survey event that Delighted's own Salesforce integration had already created for every response. That event carried the full response data but was not available quickly enough to feed the Slack alert, which is why the response was assembled from the webhooks instead. When the client was identified, the case was populated with their account information, and if they had a customer success manager, that manager received a Slack direct message. Follow-on workflows updated the case as the client acted, recording whether a meeting email had gone out and whether the client responded. Everything was linked: the Slack alert linked to the case, and the case linked to the survey event and the client record, so a support manager reached full context, including the client's other support history, in one or two clicks.
6. **Reach out right away.** Every respondent, matched or not, received a follow-up email intended to make sure they felt heard by a person and not lost in a queue. It was always sent from the support mailbox, because this was a support matter, and it offered a Calendly link. Matched clients with a customer success manager were tied to that manager. Matched clients without one, and unidentified respondents, went to a Calendly round robin of support managers, so the booking landed with the next manager who had an open slot. The email language came from the head of client support, with suggested wording from me for a couple of the messages, approved by her.
7. **Run the stateful follow-up sequence.** Each detractor had a record in a Supabase table tracking their phase: not yet emailed, first email sent, second sent, third sent, or booked. A cron-scheduled n8n workflow ran each morning during business hours, queried for records that had not booked and were due for their next step, and sent the matching email. That covered detractors who arrived overnight or on weekends, as well as follow-ups at about three days and then about a week after the first email. A booking dropped the client out of the sequence automatically. Roughly 10 to 15% of detractors booked a call. If none of the three touches produced a booking, the case went dormant until the client's next survey, which went out about every 90 days across tens of thousands of clients.

## Key Design Decisions

- **Treat the webhook stream as one response.** A Supabase record merged events field by field behind a 30-minute settling timer, so the workflow only acted on the final, assembled response.
- **Keep the existing trigger.** Zapier stayed as the webhook source to avoid building a new integration for a marginal gain.
- **Assemble first, then alert.** The Slack post waited for the settling timer so it could show the client's full comments, but it did not depend on the client match.
- **Layer the matching from certain to loose.** Domain and exact email were trusted outright, while fuzzier name logic only produced candidates.
- **Never leave an unmatched respondent unanswered.** Unknown respondents got the same outreach and a support manager round robin.
- **Track state in a database and sweep on a schedule.** Supabase recorded each detractor's phase, so the daily cron job sent only what was due, never double-sent, and dropped anyone who booked.
- **Cap the follow-ups.** Three touches over about ten days, then dormant, so the system was persistent without becoming a nuisance.
- **Link everything.** The Slack alert, the case, the survey event, and the client record were all one or two clicks apart.
- **Let support own the wording and the conversation.** The email language came from the head of client support, and the automation's scope ended at the booked meeting.

## Tech Stack

n8n for orchestration, matching, and scheduled follow-ups. Delighted for NPS surveys, with Zapier as the webhook trigger. Salesforce for client lookup and escalation cases. Supabase as the state store. Slack for the team channel and direct messages. Calendly for booking, with a support manager round robin. A support mailbox as the sender of record.
